# How Paul Graham thinks

## What they optimize for
- **Make something people want, because users are the real test.** He says a startup is where gaming the system stops working: you prosper only to the extent users want what you made, so impress users, not investors. (said in 3 sources) See [[startups/fundamentals/playing-house-and-gaming-the-system]].
  - "So, you have to have something people want. And you only prosper to the extend that you do." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 12:43; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Fundraising is merely a necessary evil. The people you need to impress are users, not investors, and the way you impress them is with prototypes, not words." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "The key to starting a successful startup is to understand some group of users so well that you can make exactly what they want." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Curiosity over careerism.** He says you can only do something exceptionally well if you are interested in it, so your taste in problems is the best guide to what to work on. (said in 3 sources) See [[founders/great-work/how-to-do-great-work]] and [[founders/choosing-work/follow-problems-to-find-golden-ages]].
  - "to do something exceptionally well, you have to be interested in it. Mere diligence is not enough." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "If you're smart and ambitious and honest with yourself, there's no better guide than your taste in problems." ([[web-35ed22df6265]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "if you're interested in genuinely interesting problems, gratifying your interest energetically is ... the best way to prepare yourself for a start-up." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 27:57; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Work whose returns compound.** Returns for performance are superlinear, so he values work where doing well makes you do better next time, and judges growth by rate, not size. (said in 3 sources) See [[startups/fundamentals/superlinear-returns]].
  - "Whenever how well you do depends on how well you've done, you'll get exponential growth." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "A couple million and 93% growth are not, in fact, radically different from a billion. They're nine and a half months apart." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "If your growth rate is good enough, the absolute numbers will take care of themselves. And the way to get the fastest growth rate is to sell to the customers who grow the fastest and decide the fastest." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Making good new things.** Besides helping people and the world, he thinks the thing to do is make good new things, and that newness matters: repeating yourself is not impressive. See [[founders/great-work/what-should-one-do]].
  - "One should help people, and take care of the world. Those two are obvious. But is there anything else? When I ask that, the answer that pops up is Make good new things." ([[web-3c8cb136189b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "it's not impressive to make the same thing over and over, however well; you're just copying yourself." ([[web-3c8cb136189b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Criticism seems sophisticated, and making new things often seems awkward, especially at first; and yet it's precisely those first steps that are most rare and valuable." ([[web-3c8cb136189b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Wealth through empathy, not exploitation.** He says a weak young startup can only win by making customers' lives better, and that squeezing customers pays far less than making them something new. See [[startups/fundamentals/can-you-earn-a-billion-without-cheating]] and [[startups/strategy/playing-the-long-game-and-being-generous]].
  - "if you want to start a successful startup, the key is not exploitation but empathy." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Newly founded startups are too weak to force anything on anyone. The only way they can become powerful is to make customers' lives better." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Squeezing every last penny out of customers is a distraction. It gets you 2x returns at most. Whereas discovering some new thing you could make for them could easily get you 10x or 100x returns." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Learning by doing.** He thinks you learn to start a startup, or any kind of work, fastest by just starting it yourself. (said in 3 sources) See [[founders/becoming-a-founder/can-you-teach-someone-to-start-a-startup]].
  - "the best way to learn how to start a startup is to try and start it. You may not be successful, but you'll learn faster if you just do it." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 33:36; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Running a startup is incompatible with being a full time student. The only way to learn how to start a startup is to do it." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "You don't necessarily need a job doing x in order to work on x; often you can just start doing it in some form yourself." ([[web-7254f259673a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Always learning powerful things.** He reduces advice for young founders to "just learn", because learning is how you end up on a path with superlinear returns. (said in 3 sources) See [[founders/becoming-a-founder/how-to-prepare-to-be-a-founder]].
  - "the ultimate advice for young would-be startup founders reduced to two words, just learn." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 30:49; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "always be learning. If you're not learning, you're probably not on a path that leads to superlinear returns." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "The best way to describe what would-be founders should study is that they should seek out powerful ideas." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)

## Rules they decide by
- **Ask for the growth rate first.** He asks founders about growth rate before anything else, because it shows whether they built the right thing and steers the company toward exponential growth. See [[growth/rate/focus-on-growth-rate]].
  - "that's the other reason I always begin by asking founders their growth rate. It shows whether they've built the right thing." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "you can use growth rate as a compass to tell you how to evolve the company. But the main advantage is that by focusing on growth rate you tend to get something that grows exponentially." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Don't hunt for startup ideas; notice them.** Ideas you consciously look for are bad but plausible, and the best ones are outliers your conscious mind would reject, so work on side projects instead. (said in 3 sources) See [[startups/ideas/how-to-get-startup-ideas]].
  - "if you make a conscious effort to try and think of startup ideas, you will think of ideas that are not only bad, but bad and plausible sounding." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 24:34; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "the best ideas tend to be such outliers that you'd overlook them if you were consciously looking for ways to make money." ([[web-7254f259673a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "the very best startup ideas tend to sound so lame, at first, that you'd reject them if you were consciously looking for startup ideas. That's what has prevented them from being discovered." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Young founders should build what they themselves want.** They lack the experience to know what others need, and what you need is expertise in your own users. See [[startups/ideas/solve-your-own-problem]].
  - "usually young founders should make something that they themselves want. You don't have enough experience yet to know what other people need." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "What you need to know to suceed in a startup is not expertise in startups, what you need is expertise in your own users." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 5:47; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Go where the best people are.** You become like the people around you, so choose work and places by who you'll be among. (said in 3 sources) See [[startups/location/should-you-move-to-silicon-valley]].
  - "One useful trick for judging different kinds of work is to look at who your colleagues will be. You'll become like whoever you work with." ([[web-7254f259673a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "the biggest advantage of moving to one of these centers is not what it does for you, but what it does to you." ([[yt-QHJkUw31YX8]] @ 8:19; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Go where interesting problems are, and you'll probably find that other smart and ambitious people have turned up there too." ([[web-35ed22df6265]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Pick cofounders like friends, and test them by working together.** Many people can seem likeable for a while, so pick people you've known long enough, and the only sure test is working with them. See [[hiring/cofounders/choosing-cofounders-and-early-hires]].
  - "Work with people you genuinely like and respect ... and that you have known long enough to be sure. Because there's a lot of people who are really good at seeming likeable for a while." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 4:38; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "the only way to tell for sure if someone will be good to work with is to work with them. Apple and Microsoft were just the last of many projects their founders had worked on together." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Ask what would make the company more powerful.** He prefers this to asking how to make more money, which yields only incremental gains, and looks for ways to move upstream and add network effects. See [[startups/strategy/making-a-startup-more-powerful]], [[startups/strategy/owning-the-customer-relationship]] and [[startups/strategy/inducing-network-effects]].
  - "One of the most useful heuristics I have when doing office hours with startups is to ask: what would make this company more powerful?" ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Upstream is almost always good, whether it's with money or user relationship or customer stage or data." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Network effects make companies more powerful, and I almost always think about how to introduce them." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Sell to customers who are early and decide fast.** Fast deciders judge you on how good you are, and big companies are just the same companies later. See [[startups/strategy/which-customers-to-sell-to]].
  - "customers who decide fast tend to decide based on how good you are." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "targeting bigger companies is just targeting a given company later." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "early adopters who decide quickly" ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]] @ 17:44; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Sell cheap at first, but not so cheap that payment stops meaning anything.** Low prices win users, but if you give away too much, growth no longer tells you whether people value the product. See [[product/pricing/charging-from-the-start]].
  - "I generally advise startups to sell as cheaply as they need to at first; get all the users, then worry about your margins." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "If your product is a $10 bill that you sell for $5, your growth rate isn't telling you anything useful." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **When unsure, keep options open and take risks early.** Uncertainty means you should choose options that lead to more options, and luck means you need several shots. See [[founders/choosing-work/deciding-what-to-work-on]] and [[founders/becoming-a-founder/who-should-aim-for-high-variance-outcomes]].
  - "The less sure you are about what to do, the more important it is to choose options that give you more options in the future. I call this "staying upwind."" ([[web-7254f259673a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Take as much risk as you can afford; if you're not failing occasionally you're probably being too conservative." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "The solution is to take multiple shots. Which is another reason to start taking risks early." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Hold the goal fixed and the means loose.** He says the persistent stay attached to the goal and give way on details low in the decision tree, while the obstinate cling to their ideas about how to get there. See [[founders/traits/persistence-vs-obstinacy]] and [[founders/traits/what-makes-someone-persistent]].
  - "The persistent are attached to the goal. The obstinate are attached to their ideas about how to reach it." ([[web-39f89fad2603]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "You'll give way on any point low down in the decision tree, if its expected value drops sufficiently, but energy and resilience keep pushing you toward whatever you chose higher up." ([[web-39f89fad2603]]; by: pg; conf: high)

## How they reason
- **Distrusts intuition about startups.** He says startups are so counterintuitive that following your instincts leads you astray, so good advice will often seem wrong. See [[startups/fundamentals/why-startup-advice-feels-counterintuitive]].
  - "startups are so weird that if you follow your instincts they will lead you astray." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 2:17; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "that's the thing about counterintuitive ideas, they contradict your intuitions, so they seem wrong." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 2:53; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Reduces outcomes to the math of growth and thresholds.** He explains big results with exponential growth and thresholds, and uses the numbers to answer moral claims, such as that billionaires must cheat. See [[startups/fundamentals/superlinear-returns]].
  - "as far as I can tell they reduce to two fundamental causes: exponential growth and thresholds." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "If your product is only half as good as your competitor's, you don't get half as many customers. You get no customers, and you go out of business." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "you don't have to cheat to become a billionaire. You've seen for yourselves that there are only two numbers in the calculation, the growth rate and how long it continues." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Checks evidence for selection bias.** He flags it in popular advice and in his own data. See [[founders/choosing-work/should-you-follow-your-passion]] and [[startups/location/building-a-startup-hub-outside-the-valley]].
  - "There's a lot of selection bias in advice about whether to "follow your passion," and this is the reason." ([[web-7254f259673a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "there's a lot of selection bias in this. It's not just measuring the effect that Silicon Valley has on startups. It's measuring also the confidence and the determination of the founders" ([[yt-QHJkUw31YX8]] @ 18:57; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Splits things that look alike.** He separates persistence from obstinacy, high prices from a bubble, and founder mode from manager mode, then reasons about each. (said in 3 sources) See [[fundraising/market/is-it-a-bubble]].
  - "The persistent are like boats whose engines can't be throttled back. The obstinate are like boats whose rudders can't be turned." ([[web-39f89fad2603]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "There is a difference between prices merely being high and a bubble." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 36:27; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "In effect there are two different ways to run a company: founder mode and manager mode." ([[web-9dafad04fa97]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Thinks by writing, and treats how prose sounds as a test of the ideas.** He says some thinking can only be done by writing, and fixing bad-sounding sentences helps get the ideas right. (said in 3 sources) See [[founders/writing/does-good-sounding-writing-mean-good-ideas]].
  - "Yes, it's bad. The reason is something I mentioned earlier: writing is thinking. In fact there's a kind of thinking that can only be done by writing." ([[web-14f0078fb455]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Fixing sentences that sound bad seems to help get the ideas right." ([[web-6c91c36a25f1]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "trying to express what you understand is not just a way to prove that you understand it, but a way to understand it better." ([[web-3c8cb136189b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Treats the fundamentals as stable and tricks as minor.** He expects startups to look the same whatever the technology, and ranks tricks far below solving the real problem. See [[startups/ai/does-ai-change-how-startups-work]].
  - "Most of starting a startup is always the same, right?" ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]] @ 0:33; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "the answer is so far almost everything is exactly the same." ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]] @ 16:38; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "There are tricks in the startups, as there are in any domain. But they are an order of magnitude less important than solving the real problem." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 13:17; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Says plainly what he doesn't know.** He admits when something can't be predicted or isn't understood yet. (said in 3 sources) See [[founders/becoming-a-founder/can-you-tell-if-youre-cut-out-for-it]] and [[founders/running-the-company/how-founder-mode-works]].
  - "You can't tell." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 21:47; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "So is it a good idea, or a bad one? We still don't know. That's how little we know about founder mode." ([[web-9dafad04fa97]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "I've never really been clear what what a lean startup is." ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]] @ 10:30; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Transforms an idea to understand it.** He asks hypothetical questions, like whether a side feature is the real product, because even failed transformations help you understand the idea. See [[product/users/when-users-misuse-your-product]].
  - "So whenever founders build something peripheral to the main product I always ask: could this be the real product?" ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "These hypothetical transformations of the original idea don't always yield anything promising. Far from it. But they're always worth considering; if nothing else, trying to transform an idea helps you understand it better." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "What would the perfect world look like, from the customer's point of view? If there's a component of that world that the startup could transform itself into, it probably should." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)

## Strong views
- **Against: growth hacks and playing house.** Going through the motions of a startup neglects the one essential thing, making something people want. See [[growth/tactics/growth-hacks]].
  - "Whenever you hear anybody talk about growth hacks, just mentally translate it in your mind to bullshit." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 10:26; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "they have neglected the one thing that's actually essential which is to make something people want." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 7:32; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Against: business school and entrepreneurship classes as founder training.** They teach management and fundraising, but the hard part of a startup is product. See [[founders/becoming-a-founder/how-to-prepare-to-be-a-founder]].
  - "what business school was designed for is to teach people management, right? And management is a problem that you only have in a startup if you're sufficiently successful." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 33:05; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "The hard part of startups is product: knowing what to build, and being able to build it. And that kind of knowledge comes from studying computer science or mechanical engineering or molecular biology, not management or finance." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "This kind of exercise is not merely useless but positively misleading. It trains founders to think that fundraising is the essential step in starting a startup" ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Against: manager mode and hired managers.** He says founders' reports show it often means hiring fakers, and hired CEOs take the company's power for granted and squeeze profit instead of making new things. See [[founders/running-the-company/founder-mode-vs-manager-mode]].
  - "Except in practice, judging from the report of founder after founder, what this often turns out to mean is: hire professional fakers and let them drive the company into the ground." ([[web-9dafad04fa97]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Hired CEOs take the power of the companies they run for granted, whereas founders remember the days when the company was so weak that it had to delight users to survive." ([[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Against: selling brand in place of substance.** Brand is what's left when real differences disappear, and it works on the worst features of human psychology, so it is a bad problem to work on. See [[product/brand/when-products-compete-on-brand]] and [[product/brand/is-branding-compatible-with-good-design]].
  - "Brand is what's left when the substantive differences between products disappear. But making the substantive differences between products disappear is what technology naturally tends to do." ([[web-35ed22df6265]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "The constraints imposed by brand ultimately depend on some of the worst features of human psychology. So when you have a world defined only by brand, it's going to be a weird, bad world." ([[web-35ed22df6265]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "pushing people's brand buttons is just not a good problem to work on, and it's hard to do good work without a good problem." ([[web-35ed22df6265]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Against: choosing work for prestige or pay.** He says a startup is a poor way to seem cool, and that choosing work for status or money puts you among people who chose it for the same reason. (said in 3 sources) See [[founders/becoming-a-founder/is-a-startup-a-good-credential]].
  - "If you want to seem cool, like starting a startup is just about the least efficient way to do it." ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]] @ 8:52; by: pg; conf: medium)
  - "Parents who pressure their kids to work on something prestigious, like medicine, even though they have no interest in it, will be hosing them even more than they have in the past." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "If you choose a kind of work mainly for how well it pays, you'll be surrounded by other people who chose it for the same reason" ([[web-7254f259673a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **For: big fortunes as honest results of growth.** He says exponential growth produces outcomes that look impossible, so unequal outcomes don't imply cheating. See [[startups/fundamentals/can-you-earn-a-billion-without-cheating]].
  - "Exponential growth is like magic. It generates outcomes that seem impossible. And that's why some politicians distrust it." ([[web-278ac10fa14b]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Superlinear returns imply inequality. The steeper the return curve, the greater the variation in outcomes." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **For: going to Silicon Valley, at least for a while.** He says it has the best peers, serendipitous meetings and a culture of helping. See [[startups/location/should-you-move-to-silicon-valley]].
  - "Should I go there? And the answer is the same now as it always is. Yes, you should. You can go there for a bit and then come back, but you should at least go." ([[yt-QHJkUw31YX8]] @ 0:36; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "In Silicon Valley, people help you for no reason." ([[yt-QHJkUw31YX8]] @ 12:15; by: pg; conf: high)
- **For: learning hard skills even when AI can do them.** Writing is thinking, and someone directing AI coders still has to be able to do the work. See [[founders/writing/should-you-still-learn-to-write]].
  - "So a world divided into writes and write-nots is more dangerous than it sounds. It will be a world of thinks and think-nots." ([[web-14f0078fb455]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "even if you have AIs writing all your code for you, you're still in the position of an engineering manager, and good engineering managers should be able to do the work of those working for them." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **For: backing founders over ideas.** Ideas change, so he looks at the founders: people who are good at building things and have a habit of doing it. See [[founders/traits/what-makes-a-founder-formidable]].
  - "startups' ideas are very mutable" ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]] @ 20:27; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "They want people who are good at building things and have a habit of doing it." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)

## Where they've changed their mind
- **Work at a company first (before 2014, reversed by 2014-09).** He used to tell would-be founders to work for another company for a few years first. In 2014 he called that a mistake, and in 2026 he still says the only way to learn is to do it. See [[tensions]].
  - "One of the things I got wrong early on, is that I advised people who were interested in starting a startup to go work for some other company for a few years before starting their own." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 33:36; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Startups in college (2014-09 vs 2026-08): a shift in emphasis, not a reversal.** In 2014 he told students to wait. In 2026, writing to universities, he wants students given free time so side projects can become startups. Both times he says a startup and full-time study don't mix. See [[tensions]].
  - "Do not start a startup in college." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 18:56; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Merely eliminating it for a few weeks resulted in two trillion dollar companies. Imagine what the US GDP would be if reading period at Harvard were twice as long." ([[web-c75e015ff81a]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Brand (2023-10 vs 2026-03): probably two senses of the word.** In 2023 a growing brand was an example of work that compounds. In 2026 he says to avoid buying or selling brand. He doesn't reconcile the two. See [[tensions]].
  - "It can compound directly, in the sense that doing well in one cycle causes you to do better in the next." ([[web-76d87c7c2e80]]; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "One obvious lesson is to stay away from brand. Indeed it's probably a good idea not just to avoid buying brand, but to avoid selling it too." ([[web-35ed22df6265]]; by: pg; conf: high)
- **Spotting founders early (2014-09 vs 2026-09): not a clear reversal.** In 2014 he stressed how little anyone can predict about who becomes tough and ambitious. In 2026 he says ambition is mostly inborn and formidable founders are usually recognizable at the first meeting. See [[tensions]].
  - "I learned from experience to keep a completely open mind about which startup in each batch, which startups would turn out to be the stars." ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]] @ 22:52; by: pg; conf: high)
  - "Sam Altman famously was extremely formidable the first time I met him before we even accepted him." ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]] @ 7:11; by: pg; conf: high)

## Blind spots
- **Stake in YC.** His advice to students and to universities comes from YC's founder, who calls YC the right lab class for startups ([[yt-ii1jcLg-eIQ]]; [[web-c75e015ff81a]]). His case for going to Silicon Valley via YC is talking his own book, which he flags himself ([[yt-QHJkUw31YX8]]). His defense of YC against "jumped the shark" came on YC's own channel ([[yt-5bxp78i96S8]]). See [[profile]].
- **YC's own numbers, given without method.** The unicorn odds for startups that go home, the count of about 30 billionaire founders, and Harvard vs Yale and Princeton application rates are YC data ([[yt-QHJkUw31YX8]]; [[web-278ac10fa14b]]; [[web-c75e015ff81a]]). See [[profile]].
- **Examples from his own portfolio.** His model founders and strategies often come from YC companies: the Collisons and Stripe, Airbnb's Chesky, OpenAI, and YC itself as a network-effects example ([[web-39f89fad2603]]; [[web-9dafad04fa97]]; [[web-19ac0b6d18d2]]; [[yt-5bxp78i96S8]]). - **Outsider on watches.** His history of the Swiss watch industry is built from ads, sales figures and a business history, plus his taste as a fan; he hasn't worked in the industry ([[web-35ed22df6265]]). See [[profile]].
